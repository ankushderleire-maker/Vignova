How Many Jobs Should You Apply To? (And How to Track Them Properly)

The trade-off between application volume and tailoring quality, a realistic weekly target, and how to run a job application tracker that lasts.

Two pieces of advice circulate constantly, and they contradict each other. One says apply to everything, because it is a numbers game. The other says apply to five carefully chosen roles, because quality beats quantity.

Both are half right, and the half that is missing is what most people get wrong.

The actual trade-off

Applications have two costs: the time to send one, and the attention to send a good one. Volume and quality trade against each other only because tailoring is slow. Change how long tailoring takes and the trade-off moves.

If a tailored application takes 45 minutes, ten a week is a part-time job, and quality collapses beyond that. If it takes ten minutes, you can send far more without any of them becoming generic.

This is why "volume or quality?" is the wrong question. The right one is: what is the largest number of genuinely tailored applications you can sustain each week?

A realistic weekly target

There is no research-backed ideal number. As a rough guide, for most people running an active search alongside other commitments:

  • 15 to 25 applications a week is a sustainable range when tailoring is fast.
  • 5 to 8 a week is a more honest ceiling if you are tailoring by hand from scratch each time.
  • Above 40 a week almost always means the applications have stopped being tailored, whatever you tell yourself.

Response rates vary enormously by field, seniority and market conditions, so treat any specific conversion figure you read with suspicion. What is consistent is the shape: a smaller number of well-matched, well-tailored applications tends to do better than a large number of generic ones, and the gap widens the more competitive the role.

Why blanket applying underperforms

Popular postings routinely attract hundreds of applicants within days. In that pool, a generic resume is not competing on merit; it is competing on whether a recruiter's search happens to surface it, which it usually does not. As covered in the 75% rejection myth, applications disappear because of volume and search, not because a robot rejected them.

There is also a self-inflicted cost. Applying to forty roles you are not qualified for generates forty silences, and forty silences is how people conclude the market is impossible and stop applying. Targeting is partly a morale strategy.

Where to draw the line on fit

A reasonable filter before you spend any time tailoring:

  • Do you meet most of the required qualifications? Not all, most. Postings are wish lists, and many employers interview candidates who do not tick every box.
  • Is there a genuine overlap between your history and the core of the posting? If nothing in your background maps to the main requirements, that is not a resume problem that better tailoring can fix.
  • Would you take the job? Location, pay, work model and notice period. Applying to roles you would decline wastes the scarcest resource in a job search: your own attention.

If you find yourself failing the second test repeatedly, the useful move is to change what you are applying for, not to apply harder. Running a posting through an ATS resume checker answers this quickly: a low semantic score against your genuine history means "wrong role", not "worse resume". The guide to reading an ATS score explains the bands.

Running a tracker that survives

Everyone starts with a spreadsheet. Many people abandon it after a few weeks, at exactly the point where it starts to matter: when interviews begin and you can no longer remember which of six resume versions went to which company.

What a tracker has to hold to be worth maintaining:

Which resume version you sent. The single most important field, and the one spreadsheets handle worst. When a recruiter calls about a role you applied for three weeks ago, you need the exact document in front of you before you speak.

Stage, not just status. Saved, Applied, Interview, Offer and Rejected. A board view makes the shape of your pipeline visible at a glance, including the uncomfortable truth when twenty cards are stuck in Applied.

The job description itself. Postings get taken down. Preparing for an interview against a job description you can no longer read is avoidable pain, so save the text when you apply.

Key dates. When you applied, any application deadline and the interview date, for follow-up timing and for noticing that a company has gone quiet.

Contact and source. Whether a role came from Naukri, LinkedIn, a referral, a recruiter or a company's careers page changes how you follow up.

Vignova's job application tracker keeps each tailored resume and cover letter with the job it was written for, which takes most of the pain out of version tracking. A spreadsheet can work too; it just demands discipline exactly when you have least of it.

A weekly rhythm that works

A structure that holds up better than applying in bursts:

  • Monday: source. Collect the postings that pass your fit filter. Save them; do not apply yet. Separating sourcing from applying stops you drifting into low-fit roles when the list looks thin.
  • Tuesday to Thursday: tailor and send. Work through the saved list. Tailoring in a batch is faster than one at a time because you stay in the same mode.
  • Friday: follow up and review. Chase anything that has been silent for two weeks. Look at what got responses and adjust what you are targeting.

The point of the rhythm is that it makes the search finite. An open-ended "apply whenever" search expands to fill every evening and burns people out quickly.

When to follow up

Roughly 10 to 14 days after applying, once, briefly, and to a named person if you can find one. A second follow-up rarely helps and occasionally hurts.

After an interview, send a short thank-you within 24 hours, then follow the timeline they gave you. If they said two weeks, follow up on day 15, not day 8.

Frequently asked questions

Is it worth applying to a role posted three weeks ago?

Often yes for less competitive roles, less often for high-volume ones where a shortlist may already exist. Prioritise postings under a week old.

Should I apply to several roles at the same company?

Two, if they are genuinely different and you are genuinely qualified for both. Five reads as spraying.

Does applying early matter?

For high-volume roles it can matter a lot, because shortlists form fast. This is the strongest argument for a sourcing routine that catches postings within a few days.

How do I stay organised across multiple resume versions?

Attach the resume to the job record rather than storing resumes in a folder. The link between document and application is the thing you actually need to preserve; see how to tailor for each role without losing track.

The short version

Do not choose between volume and quality; reduce the cost of quality until you can afford volume. A steady number of genuinely tailored applications each week, filtered for real fit and tracked with the resume attached to the role, beats both the five-application purist and the hundred-application sprayer.
