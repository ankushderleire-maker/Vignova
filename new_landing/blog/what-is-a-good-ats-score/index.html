What Is a Good ATS Score? How to Read a Resume Match Score

There is no universal passing ATS score. What a resume match score measures, sensible targets for a job you fit, and when a higher score is not worth chasing.

You run your resume through an ATS checker and get 64. Is that good? Should you fix it before applying, or send it as it is? The honest answer starts with an uncomfortable fact: there is no official passing score.

This guide explains what the number measures, what targets make sense for a job you are qualified for, and when chasing a higher score makes your resume worse.

The short answer

For a job that genuinely fits your experience, aim for a score your checker describes as good or better. In Vignova, that means 70 or above. Below 50 usually means the resume has not been tailored to this job, or the job is not a close fit. Above 80, extra points rarely matter as much as how the resume reads to a person.

Two things matter more than any single number: the score belongs to one resume against one job description, and different tools calculate it differently.

Why there is no universal passing score

An applicant tracking system (ATS) is the software employers use to collect applications, search them and move candidates through hiring stages. Employers do not publish a pass mark for your resume, and recruiters describe their systems as tools for searching and sorting rather than for automatically rejecting documents. The article on the 75% rejection myth covers where the popular scare statistic came from.

The "ATS score" you see comes from a resume checker, not from the employer's system. Each checker decides what to measure and how much weight to give it, which is why the same resume can score 58 in one tool and 71 in another.

So the useful question is not "did I pass?" but "how closely does this resume match what this employer asked for, and how easy is it to read?"

What goes into the score

Vignova's ATS resume checker combines six areas. Other tools use different mixes, but most measure some version of these.

Areas in Vignova's ATS score and their weights
AreaWeightWhat it checks
Keywords25%Skills and terms from the job description that appear in your resume
Semantics20%Whether your experience matches the meaning of the job, even when the wording differs
Sections15%Whether a summary, experience, education, skills and projects are present
Impact15%Action verbs and measurable results in your bullet points
Format15%Contact details, consistent dates, and characters or tables that can confuse parsers
Readability10%Sentence length, bullet density and overall length

Read the areas separately before you react to the total. A 62 made up of strong format and weak keywords needs different work from a 62 with good keywords and no measurable results.

How to read your score

Vignova labels scores in five bands. The labels describe how much work the resume needs for this job. They are not a prediction of whether you will be shortlisted.

Vignova ATS score bands and what to do in each
ScoreLabelWhat it usually meansWhat to do
80 and aboveExcellentClosely matched and easy to readProofread and apply. Check that it still sounds like you.
70 to 79GoodWell matched, with small gapsAdd any missing keywords that are true of you, then apply.
50 to 69FairPartly tailored, with clear gapsWork through the keywords and your weakest bullet points.

Below 30PoorA different kind of role, or a file that did not read properlyCheck the file first, then decide whether the job fits.

Why the same resume gets different scores

  • Different job descriptions. A detailed posting that names twenty tools gives the keyword check more to find than a four-line posting. Very short job descriptions produce less reliable keyword scores.
  • Different tools. Each checker weights the areas differently. Compare scores within one tool, not across tools.
  • Different versions of your resume. A resume tailored for a data analyst role will score lower against a business analyst posting, and it should.
  • A file that did not read properly. A scanned PDF or a heavily designed layout can lose text when it is extracted. If a score looks far too low, copy your resume into a plain text editor and check that everything is there.

When a lower score is fine

A score in the 60s is not automatically a reason to hold back an application.

  • You have a referral. A person will read your resume, however a search would rank it.
  • You are changing careers. Transferable experience often uses different words from the new field. Add the true keywords you can, and let your summary explain the move.
  • The posting is a wish list. If the missing terms are "preferred" rather than "required", apply when you meet the core of the role.
  • You are a fresher. Projects, internships and coursework will not match every requirement written for experienced hires. Make sure they use the posting's language wherever they genuinely can.

When a high score is a warning

A resume can score very well and still read badly. Watch for these signs:

  • A skills section with forty terms, many of which you have used once
  • Phrases copied word for word from the job description into every bullet point
  • Keywords you could not discuss confidently in an interview
  • A summary that reads like a list of search terms

Every word on your resume is something an interviewer can ask about. A slightly lower score on a resume you can defend is worth more than a perfect score on one you cannot.

How to raise your score honestly

  1. Fix reading problems first. Use a simple single-column layout with standard headings and selectable text. The ATS-friendly resume format guide covers the details.
  2. Add missing keywords that are true of you. Put them where you used them, in experience or project bullet points, not only in the skills list. The resume keywords guide explains how.
  3. Rewrite your three weakest bullet points so they show what you did and what changed as a result.
  4. Update your summary with the two or three terms that define the role.
  5. Check again, then stop. Moving from 76 to 81 is rarely worth another hour. Sending the application is.

For job seekers in India

  • Paste the complete job description, including any key skills listed with the posting. Those are often the terms the recruiter selected to search for.
  • Keep one base resume per kind of role, such as one for data analyst roles and one for MIS reporting roles, and tailor from there.
  • Line up your profiles too. Recruiters who find you on Naukri or LinkedIn will compare your profile with your resume. The Naukri headline examples show how to keep them consistent.

Frequently asked questions

Is 60 a good ATS score?

In Vignova, 60 falls in the Fair band: the resume matches part of the job, with clear gaps. If the role fits you, spend a few minutes on missing keywords and your weakest bullet points before you apply.

Should I aim for 100%?

No. Getting close to 100 usually means repeating the job description, which makes the resume read worse to the person who decides.

Does a high ATS score guarantee an interview?

No. A good score means your resume matches the posting and is easy to read. Interviews depend on the other applicants, the employer's needs and timing.

Why did my score change when I edited only one section?

Each area has its own weight. Adding a missing projects section or fixing inconsistent dates can move the total noticeably, even when the rest of the resume is unchanged.

The short version

There is no passing ATS score. Treat the number as a measure of how well one resume matches one job. For a role you fit, aim for Good or Excellent, which in Vignova means 70 or more. Fix reading problems and honest keyword gaps first, and stop before the resume starts reading like the job description.
