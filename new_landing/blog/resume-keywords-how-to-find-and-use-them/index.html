Resume Keywords: How to Find the Right Ones and Use Them Properly

Where resume keywords come from, which ones are worth including, where to place them so they count, and how to avoid keyword stuffing that makes a recruiter close the file.

Resume keywords get discussed as though they were a trick. They are not. They are simply the words a recruiter types into their applicant tracking system when they need to find five candidates out of six hundred.

Getting them right decides whether you appear in that result set. Getting them wrong — usually by cramming — decides whether the recruiter keeps reading once you do.

Where resume keywords actually come from

There is no universal list. Your keywords come from one place: the job description in front of you.

Generic "top 500 resume keywords" articles are close to useless for this reason. The terms that matter for a Senior Data Engineer role at a logistics company are not the terms that matter for the same title at a bank. Anything general enough to appear on every list is general enough to be worthless as a search term.

A single posting typically contains 15 to 30 real keywords. Roughly a third will be genuinely true of you, and those are the ones worth acting on.

The five types worth extracting

Read the posting twice. On the second pass, sort what you find into these buckets.

Hard skills, tools and technologies

Named things: Python, Snowflake, Figma, HIPAA, IFRS, Salesforce, Kubernetes. These are the highest-value keywords by a wide margin, because they are unambiguous and recruiters search for them literally.

They are also the least negotiable. A recruiter searching for "Terraform" will not find "infrastructure as code".

Job titles

The posting's title, plus any variants in the body text. If your title differs but the work was the same, bridge the gap honestly: "Business Intelligence Analyst (Data Analyst)" is accurate and searchable. Inventing a title you never held is not.

Methodologies and frameworks

Agile, Scrum, Six Sigma, RICE prioritisation, OKRs, design sprints, MLOps. These tell a recruiter how you work, and they are common search filters for roles where process fit matters.

Certifications and qualifications

PMP, CPA, AWS Solutions Architect, CFA, a specific licence, a specific degree requirement. These are frequently used as hard filters — and often appear as knockout questions on the form itself.

Domain and industry language

The vocabulary of the field: churn, underwriting, clinical trials, supply chain visibility, unit economics, safety-critical systems. These separate someone who has worked in the domain from someone describing it from the outside.

What to ignore

Skip the boilerplate. Fast-paced environment, team player, self-starter, passionate, detail-oriented. No recruiter searches for these, they consume space that could hold evidence, and they signal filler.

Prioritising: not every keyword deserves a place

Once you have the list, sort it:

  1. Required + true of you. Non-negotiable. These must appear, ideally more than once and at least once inside your work experience.
  2. Repeated three or more times in the posting. Repetition is the employer telling you what the role is really about.
  3. Preferred + true of you. Worth including where they fit naturally.
  4. True of you but not mentioned. Leave out unless it is genuinely impressive. Space is scarce.
  5. Not true of you. Leave out. Always. A keyword you cannot defend in an interview is a liability, not an asset.

That fifth category is the whole ethical line of keyword optimization, and it is also the practical one. Every term on your resume is an invitation to be questioned about it.

Where to place keywords so they count

Placement changes the weight a term carries — both to matching algorithms and to a human reading fast.

Professional summary — three to five priority terms. This is the highest-value real estate on the page. Write it as a sentence a person would say, not a keyword list with punctuation.

Skills section — the clean, unambiguous list. This is where a machine gets a tidy index of your capabilities. Group by category (Languages, Cloud, Analytics) rather than running a fifty-item comma soup. Twelve to twenty terms is plenty.

Work experience — where most keywords should live. A term inside a bullet comes with proof attached: what you did with it, and what happened. "Migrated 40 services to Kubernetes, cutting deploy time from 25 minutes to 4" is worth more than the word Kubernetes in a list, to both audiences.

Job titles and company lines. Often overlooked, and directly searched.

Not in headers, footers, images, or white text. Headers and footers are frequently skipped by parsers. Images are invisible to them. White text is visible to any human who selects the page, and reads as an attempt to cheat.

How many times should a keyword appear?

Enough to be unambiguous, not enough to be conspicuous. For a genuinely central skill, two or three mentions across summary, skills and one experience bullet is natural. Beyond that you are writing for a machine at the expense of a person.

There is no threshold that unlocks anything. Many matching systems actively penalise unnatural term density, and every recruiter recognises a stuffed resume on sight.

Keyword stuffing: what it looks like and why it fails

The common forms:

  • Pasting the entire job description in white text at the bottom of the page.
  • A skills section listing sixty technologies, most touched once.
  • The same phrase repeated in every bullet regardless of fit.
  • A summary that is a comma-separated keyword list wearing a sentence costume.

All of these fail for the same reason: the document is read by a human at some point. Stuffing wins nothing at the search stage and loses everything at the reading stage.

It also fails the interview test. A resume claiming sixty technologies invites the question of which ones you can actually discuss — and the answer is never sixty.

Keywords and LinkedIn

The same logic applies to your LinkedIn profile, with one difference: LinkedIn is a standing document rather than a per-application one, so it should carry the keywords for the roles you want, not the role you last held.

Recruiters search LinkedIn with the same instincts they use in an ATS. Headline, About section and the skills list are the fields that get indexed hardest. Vignova's LinkedIn optimizer scores a profile against your target roles for exactly this reason.

Frequently asked questions

Should I use the exact wording from the job description? For hard skills, tools and certifications, yes — exact matching is how search works. For everything else, natural phrasing that carries the concept is fine and reads better.

Do acronyms or full terms work better? Use both on first mention: "Search Engine Optimization (SEO)". Recruiters search either, and you cannot predict which.

Do keywords matter if I was referred? Less for search, but your resume still gets read against the posting by someone. Relevance always matters; discoverability is what a referral replaces.

How do I know which keywords I am missing? Compare manually, or run the posting and your resume through an ATS resume checker that lists missing terms explicitly. The manual version works; it just takes longer.

The short version

Keywords come from the specific posting, not a generic list. Extract the hard skills, titles, methodologies, certifications and domain language; keep the ones that are honestly yours; place most of them inside work experience where they come with evidence; and stop well before the page reads like it was written for a machine.
