Resume keywords get discussed as though they were a trick. They are not. They are simply the words a recruiter types into their applicant tracking system when they need to find a handful of candidates among hundreds.

Getting them right decides whether you appear in that result set. Getting them wrong, usually by cramming, decides whether the recruiter keeps reading once you do.

Where resume keywords actually come from

There is no universal list. Your keywords come from one place: the job description in front of you.

Generic "top 500 resume keywords" articles are close to useless for this reason. The terms that matter for a Senior Data Engineer role at a logistics company are not the terms that matter for the same title at a bank. Anything general enough to appear on every list is too general to be useful as a search term.

A detailed posting can contain a couple of dozen real keywords. Usually only some of them are genuinely true of you, and those are the ones worth acting on.

The five types worth extracting

Read the posting twice. On the second pass, sort what you find into these buckets.

Hard skills, tools and technologies

Named things: Python, Power BI, Figma, Tally Prime, GST, SAP FICO, Salesforce, Kubernetes. These are the highest-value keywords by a wide margin, because they are unambiguous and recruiters search for them literally.

They are also the least negotiable. A recruiter searching for "Terraform" will not find "infrastructure as code".

Job titles

The posting's title, plus any variants in the body text. If your title differs but the work was the same, bridge the gap honestly: "Business Intelligence Analyst (Data Analyst)" is accurate and searchable. Inventing a title you never held is not.

Methodologies and frameworks

Agile, Scrum, Six Sigma, OKRs, design sprints, MLOps. These tell a recruiter how you work, and they are common search filters for roles where process fit matters.

Certifications and qualifications

PMP, CA, CFA, AWS Solutions Architect, a specific licence or a specific degree requirement. These are frequently used as hard filters, and often appear as screening questions on the application form itself.

Domain and industry language

The vocabulary of the field: churn, underwriting, clinical trials, supply chain visibility, unit economics, safety-critical systems. These separate someone who has worked in the domain from someone describing it from the outside.

What to ignore

Skip the boilerplate: fast-paced environment, team player, self-starter, passionate, detail-oriented. Recruiters rarely search for these, they take up space that could hold evidence, and they read as filler.

Prioritising: not every keyword deserves a place

Once you have the list, sort it:

  1. Required and true of you. Non-negotiable. These must appear, ideally more than once and at least once inside your work experience.
  2. Repeated three or more times in the posting. Repetition is the employer telling you what the role is really about.
  3. Preferred and true of you. Worth including where they fit naturally.
  4. True of you but not mentioned. Leave out unless it is genuinely impressive. Space is scarce.
  5. Not true of you. Leave out. Always. A keyword you cannot defend in an interview is a liability, not an asset.

That fifth category is the whole ethical line of keyword optimization, and it is also the practical one. Every term on your resume is an invitation to be questioned about it.

Where to place keywords so they count

Placement changes how much a term tells a reader, and how convincing it is.

Professional summary: three to five priority terms. This is the most valuable space on the page. Write it as a sentence a person would say, not a keyword list with punctuation.

Skills section: the clean, unambiguous list. This is where a machine gets a tidy index of your capabilities. Group skills by category (Languages, Cloud, Analytics) rather than running a fifty-item comma soup. Twelve to twenty terms is plenty for most roles.

Work experience: where most keywords should live. A term inside a bullet point comes with proof attached: what you did with it, and what happened. "Migrated 40 services to Kubernetes, cutting deploy time from 25 minutes to 4" is worth more than the word Kubernetes in a list, to both audiences.

Job titles and company lines. Often overlooked, and directly searched.

Not in headers, footers, images or white text. Headers and footers are frequently skipped by parsers. Images are invisible to them. White text is visible to any human who selects the page, and reads as an attempt to cheat.

How many times should a keyword appear?

Enough to be unambiguous, not enough to be conspicuous. For a genuinely central skill, two or three mentions across your summary, skills and one experience bullet point is natural. Beyond that you are writing for a machine at the expense of a person.

There is no threshold that unlocks anything, and every recruiter recognises a stuffed resume on sight.

Keyword stuffing: what it looks like and why it fails

The common forms:

  • Pasting the entire job description in white text at the bottom of the page.
  • A skills section listing sixty technologies, most of them touched once.
  • The same phrase repeated in every bullet point regardless of fit.
  • A summary that is a comma-separated keyword list dressed up as a sentence.

All of these fail for the same reason: the document is read by a human at some point. Stuffing wins nothing at the search stage and loses everything at the reading stage.

It also fails the interview test. A resume claiming sixty technologies invites the question of which ones you can actually discuss, and the answer is never sixty.

Keywords on LinkedIn and Naukri

The same logic applies to your job portal profiles, with one difference: a profile is a standing document rather than a per-application one, so it should carry the keywords for the roles you want, not only the role you last held.

Frequently asked questions

Should I use the exact wording from the job description?

For hard skills, tools and certifications, yes: exact matching is how search works. For everything else, natural phrasing that carries the concept is fine and reads better.

Do acronyms or full terms work better?

Use both on first mention, such as "Search Engine Optimization (SEO)". Recruiters search for either, and you cannot predict which.

Do keywords matter if I was referred?

Less for search, but your resume still gets read against the posting by someone. Relevance always matters; discoverability is what a referral replaces.

What are good keywords for a fresher resume?

The ones in the postings you apply for: tools from your coursework, projects and internships (such as Python, AutoCAD or Tally Prime), the degree and specialisation the posting names, and relevant certifications. Put them in project and internship bullet points, not only in a skills list.

How do I know which keywords I am missing?

Paste your resume and the posting into the free resume keyword scanner, which lists matched and missing terms without uploading anything, or run a full ATS check, which extracts keywords from any posting with AI. Comparing by hand works too; it just takes longer.

The short version

Keywords come from the specific posting, not a generic list. Extract the hard skills, titles, methodologies, certifications and domain language; keep the ones that are honestly yours; place most of them inside your work experience, where they come with evidence; and stop well before the page reads like it was written for a machine.
